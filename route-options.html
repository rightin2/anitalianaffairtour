Five ways to draw the route

Same six stops, same facts. Each version puts the region names in their own space, marks how we travel each leg, and shows the Venice evening as optional. Scroll to see each one draw itself.

1

The wave, with the regions on a shelf above

Closest to what is there now. The three regions become a header strip across the top, each spanning exactly the stretch of line it covers, so the names never sit under the path. Private transfers ride the line in small cream badges; the Fiat 500s and the boat sit beside Lucca and Cinque Terre as activities.

* Venice: optional informal gathering the evening before; transfer to Asolo included, Venice costs at your own expense.

2

Three bands, one straight line

A metro-map idea. Each region is a soft sage band with its name set large at the top left, so the regions read first and the stops second. The line is dead straight and calm. Cinque Terre hangs off Lucca as a spur, which is truthful: a day out by transfer, the boat along the coast, and back to Lucca.

3

Terraces

The three regions as stepped plateaus, like hillside terraces. Each plateau carries its name in the clear, the stops sit on the flat, and the vehicles work the ramps between them. The boat drops off the Tuscan terrace to a strip of sea below.

4

Three arches

Borrowed straight from the brochure: each region is an arch-topped frame, the shape used for the photographs. The region name sits inside the arch, the stops beneath it, and the route threads through the three frames with the vehicles on the bridges between them. Most on-brand of the five.

5

The vertical ribbon

A spine that runs down the page, one region per band, the name set huge and light in the margin so it is never in the way. Stops alternate left and right, vehicles sit on the spine where one leg becomes the next, and the boat heads out to the right onto the water. Reads identically on a phone, which none of the others do without a second layout.
